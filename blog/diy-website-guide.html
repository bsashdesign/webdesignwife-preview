The Do-It-Yourself Website Guide: How to Do It Right

By Ben Sash, 7 min read

The short answer: plenty of local businesses build their own websites and do great. The key is keeping it simple, focusing on what your customers need, and keeping it current. Here's the plan I'd follow, step by step.

1. Pick a Website Builder

There's no single best builder. Pick the one that fits how you work, then commit: moving a site between builders later usually means rebuilding it. Here are the main options, with my honest comparison of each:

  • Squarespace: polished templates, great if you care about design. vs. Squarespace
  • Wix: the most drag-and-drop freedom, lots of add-on apps. vs. Wix
  • WordPress: the most flexible, but you (or someone) maintains it. vs. WordPress
  • Shopify: the best choice if you mainly sell products online. vs. Shopify
  • Square Online: simple, and a natural fit if you already use Square. vs. Square Online
  • GoDaddy Website Builder: quick to set up if your domain is already there. vs. GoDaddy Website Builder
  • Google Sites: free and basic, fine for a simple info page. vs. Google Sites
  • Carrd: very cheap one-page sites. vs. Carrd
  • Hostinger: low-cost builder with hosting included. vs. Hostinger Website Builder
  • Webflow and Framer: powerful design tools, with a learning curve. vs. Webflowvs. Framer

Step-by-step getting-started guides for each builder are on the way.

2. Get the Right Domain, in Your Own Name

Your domain (like yourbusiness.com) is your address online, and it's the one part of your website you should never let someone else own. Register it with an account in your name, using your email, even if someone else builds your site.

Where to Buy Your Domain

These registrars are all reputable. A .com usually costs about $10 to $25 a year.

  • Cloudflare Registrar: charges exactly what the domain costs, with no markup, at purchase and at renewal.
  • Namecheap
  • Porkbun
  • GoDaddy
  • Squarespace Domains (which took over Google Domains)
Watch the renewal price. Many registrars advertise a cheap first year, then charge much more to renew. Check the renewal price before you buy.
Turn on auto-renew. If your domain expires, your website and any email on that domain stop working, and someone else can register it. Once that happens, you will very likely never get it back.

Check Whether the Name Is Taken

  • Search the name on any registrar above. If it's available, you'll see a price.
  • Before you commit to a business name, search the USPTO trademark database so you don't build on a name someone else has protected.

If the .com You Want Is Taken

You can usually still get a .com by adding a word that keeps the essence of your name:

  • Add your location: rosebakerybrooklyn.com, kingscutbronx.com
  • Add what you do: riveraplumbing.com, juniperhairstudio.com
  • Add a Short Word: get, hello, shop or studio, like hellojuniper.com
  • Use your full name if the short one is taken: caldwelltaxandaccounting.com

Avoid hyphens and numbers. They're hard to say out loud and easy to mistype.

Alternatives to .com

If your business grows by word of mouth, people will type ".com" out of habit, so it's worth the effort to get one. If most customers find you on Google Maps or Instagram, the ending matters less. Good options:

  • .nyc: yes, it's real. It's New York City's official ending, and you need a city address to register one, which makes it a nice local signal.
  • .co: short and professional, though people sometimes add the "m" by mistake.
  • Industry endings like .salon, .dental, .studio or .shop: memorable, but less familiar to some customers.

3. Start Small: One Page Is Often Enough

You probably don't need a big website. You need a site that answers your customers' questions in the right order. You can always add pages later, and every extra page is one more thing to keep updated. There's no one-size-fits-all layout, but here's a starting point.

If One Page Fits Your Business

This works well for barbers, cleaners, food trucks, handymen, solo therapists and trainers, or anyone with a short list of services. Here's one way you could organize it, top to bottom:

  1. What you do and where, with one clear button: "Call now," "Book online" or "Get a quote"
  2. Your services, with prices or starting prices if you can
  3. Reviews from your customers
  4. About you, with a real photo of you or your team (see my guide to photos for your business website) Photos for Your Business Website
  5. Hours, address and a map
  6. Contact: phone, text, email or a short form, repeated at the bottom
Showing your Google reviews: use your builder's built-in Google reviews feature or an official integration, which keeps them live and current. Don't copy and paste reviews onto your site by hand: Google's terms restrict reusing review content that way.

If You Need Three to Five Pages

  • Restaurant or café: Home, Menu, Visit (hours, location, reservations), About, Contact
  • Salon or spa: Home, Services and prices, Book, About the team
  • Dentist, clinic or law office: Home, Services, About the team, Contact
  • Contractor: Home, Services, Past projects, Contact

A separate Contact page helps when you have a longer form, several locations or a booking tool. Keep your phone number on every page anyway.

4. Write for Your Customers, Not About Yourself

Your visitors skim. They're looking for their exact problem, a reason to trust you, and the fastest way to reach you. Write every section with that in mind.

  • Lead with what you do and where. "Emergency plumbing in Queens, same-day service" beats "Welcome to our website." Someone should know they're in the right place within three seconds.
  • Name your services the way customers do. Not "plumbing solutions," but "drain cleaning," "leak repair" and "water heater installation." That's also what Google matches against.
  • Talk about their problem, then your fix. "Water heater out? I can usually replace it the same day."
  • Show proof. Reviews, years in business, licenses, insurance, "family-run since 1987." Proof does more than adjectives.
  • Give prices, or at least a range. "Haircuts from $35" gets more calls than no price at all, because people assume the worst.
  • Mention your neighborhood. Cross streets, nearby landmarks and the areas you serve help locals and help Google.
  • End each section with a next step, and keep your phone number one tap away on phones.
  • Answer the questions you hear every day in a short FAQ: parking, payment, walk-ins, how long it takes.

5. Use Real Photos

Your team, your space and your work build more trust than any stock photo, and a recent phone camera is plenty. 6. Set Up Your Google Business Profile

For most local businesses, the Google Maps listing brings in as many customers as the website, often more:

  • Claim and verify it at business.google.com, using an account you own.
  • Pick the most specific main category, like "Emergency plumber" rather than "Plumber," then add a few secondary ones.
  • Keep hours exact, including holiday hours, and match them to your website.
  • Add real photos and keep adding new ones.
  • List your services with the same names you use on your website.
  • Ask for reviews after every good job, and reply to every review, good or bad.

More in my guide: Why Your Google Maps Profile Might Matter More Than Your Website.

7. Test It Properly Before You Share It

  • Test on your phone first. Tap every button and link: do call, directions and booking go to the right place?
  • Send yourself a message through your own contact form, and confirm it arrives in your inbox, not spam.
  • Check your speed with Google's free PageSpeed Insights. Large photos are the usual culprit, so shrink them before you upload.
  • Proofread your phone number, address, hours and prices twice. These are the mistakes that cost calls.
  • Ask a friend to find your hours and contact you without your help, and watch where they get stuck.

8. Keep It Current

An outdated website quietly costs you customers: wrong hours send people away, and old prices start arguments. Put a monthly reminder on your calendar to check your hours (including upcoming holidays), prices, services, photos and Google profile. It takes fifteen minutes, and it's the difference between a website that works and one that just exists.
